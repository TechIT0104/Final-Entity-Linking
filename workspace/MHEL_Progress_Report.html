Overview

One research thread, three layers of evidence

Everything in this workspace serves one thesis: Multilingual Historical Entity Linking (MHEL‑LLaMo) — linking mentions in old, multilingual documents to the correct Wikidata entity (or correctly flagging "no match," NIL). The repo holds three things at once: the reproduction of a published method this work builds on, an original extension on top of it, and a large, separate baseline-comparison and dataset-recovery effort that stress-tests both against BLINK, GENRE, mReFinED, MVD and mulrel-nel on Mewsli‑9 and TR2016.

The reproduced method comes from "It's All About the Confidence: An Unsupervised Approach for Multilingual Historical Entity Linking using Large Language Models" (Santini, van Erp, Alam — EACL 2026). Its pipeline: a multilingual bi‑encoder called BELA retrieves candidate Wikidata entities per mention, a confidence score decides whether the mention is "easy" (take BELA's top‑1) or "hard" (hand it to an LLM — Mistral‑Small‑24B, Llama‑Poro‑2‑8B, or Gemma‑3‑27B depending on language), and the LLM performs NIL detection and final candidate selection through prompt chaining, entirely without fine‑tuning.

Core system

What was reproduced, and what was added on top

The base pipeline was reproduced faithfully: 12 of 12 published per-dataset F1 scores across HIPE‑2020 (en/de/fr), NewsEye (de/fi/fr/sv), AJMC (en/de/fr) and MHERCL (en/it) matched the paper within a ±0.01 tolerance — average 0.595 reproduced vs. 0.608 published. That reproduction alone is a meaningful chunk of engineering: getting a 2021‑era bi‑encoder and a 24B‑parameter LLM to co‑exist on a single 32GB GPU required mixed precision, gradient accumulation, and Flash Attention.

On top of that reproduction, this project's own declared contribution (framed in its docs as roughly 70% paper-inspired, 30% original) is:

XGBoost confidence router

Trained on 18,075 mention samples using top‑1 confidence, score margin, mention length and Levenshtein distance as features. Reaches 82% routing accuracy against a 70% fixed‑threshold baseline — beating logistic regression (74%), random forest (78%), gradient boosting (79%) and a neural net (80%) in the same comparison. Shipped as a 50KB deployable model.

Systematic error analysis

On AJMC‑EN (baseline 47.0% accuracy, F1 ≈44%), found abbreviations cause ~40% of false positives, 59% of false positives are NIL‑misclassifications, and WORK‑type entities are the hardest category — the kind of diagnosis that explains why the numbers are what they are, not just what they are.

RAG / ensemble extension

A cross‑encoder reranker, a context‑augmentation step, and a tunable retrieval/reranker/LLM‑confidence ensemble (default weights 0.3/0.4/0.3) were built and a deployment bug fixed (an IndexError in the retriever from mismatched valid/invalid mention indices). Expected to add roughly +3–8% F1 on AJMC‑EN and +2–5% on NewsEye‑FI — this run was scripted and staged but its completion isn't confirmed in the current result files, so treat it as in progress, not banked.

Measurement honesty pass

A second, stricter scoring pass ("honest" tables) re-derives F1 by joining strictly on document/position and accounting for gold mentions the pipeline silently failed to predict at all (coverage as low as 92.8% on NewsEye‑FI). Under that stricter lens, most scores drop slightly (e.g. HIPE‑EN 0.723 → 0.708) — the kind of self-audit that most reproduction efforts skip.

Engineering

Fitting a 24B model where a paper assumed 96GB

The 96GB-vs-32GB framing shows up explicitly in the project's own slides (final_presentation.py, professional_balanced_pptx.py): "Mistral‑24B requires 96GB in FP32, we have 32GB GPU." That 96GB is the project's own back‑of‑envelope estimate (24B params × 4 bytes/param at FP32), not a number the original paper states — but the constraint it points at is real: a 24‑billion‑parameter model does not fit on a single consumer/workstation GPU at full precision, and the actual hardware available was a lot smaller than that.

The documented progression (research diary, EXPERIMENTAL_VARIATIONS.md) walks through the standard levers in order, with the memory footprint measured at each step:

ConfigurationFootprintOutcome
FP32 (paper-equivalent)~96 GBOOM on 32GB card
FP16~48 GBstill OOM
8‑bit quantization~24 GBfits, slow
4‑bit quantization~12 GBfits, fast
Mixed precision + gradient accumulation (selected)~28 GB4GB headroom on 32GB card

The configuration that actually shipped combines mixed-precision autocast with gradient accumulation over 4 steps, run at batch size 128 (a batch-size sweep found 128 the sweet spot at ~28GB / 8ms latency; 256 overflowed). The real inference code (filter_and_prompt_chain.py, filter_and_prompt_rag.py) defaults to loading the LLM in bfloat16 rather than fp16 specifically, with an environment variable (MHEL_TORCH_DTYPE) to switch between fp16/bf16/fp32, plus HuggingFace Accelerate's device_map="auto" for automatic GPU/CPU offload and explicit memory caps (MHEL_MAX_GPU_MEM=20GiB, MHEL_MAX_CPU_MEM=120GiB) so the loader never overcommits a card. Interestingly, bitsandbytes is installed as a dependency but never actually invoked (no load_in_4bit/load_in_8bit calls found in the shipped scripts) — so the 4-bit/8-bit rows above look like an experiment that was tried and measured, but the version that ended up in production relies on bf16 + accumulation instead of quantization.

Hardware actually used: an RTX 5000 Ada (32GB) on one server and 4× RTX 3090 (24GB each) on another. Worth flagging: IMPLEMENTATION_DETAILS.md/EXPERIMENTAL_VARIATIONS.md read as a written research narrative rather than raw profiler logs — the numbers are internally consistent but there's no raw nvidia-smi trace backing them in the repo, so treat them as the project's documented account rather than independently re-verifiable telemetry.

On the NER-augmented Mewsli‑9 claim — I couldn't confirm this one. I searched every Mewsli-related script (convert_mewsli.py, download_mewsli.py, preprocess_mewsli.py, the mention-detection eval scripts) and grepped the whole repo for spaCy/Stanza/Flair or any "additional annotations" logic. All three Mewsli scripts are straight pass-throughs — they reformat the original mentions.tsv gold spans into HIPE/KILT-style files and print a converted-count that matches the input count 1:1. There's no step anywhere that runs a NER model over Mewsli text to add mentions beyond the original annotations, and no before/after mention-count table exists in any report. It's possible this lives only in the thesis PDF text (which wasn't machine-readable to the agent) or was done outside this repo — worth double-checking the thesis or your own notes for where that number came from before it goes in front of a supervisor.

Comparison

How the baselines actually performed

A separate, substantial effort benchmarked five external systems, mostly on Mewsli‑9, largely on rented remote GPUs (172.20.70.80 / .170). The honest picture is mixed — some baselines came close to their papers, others fell far short, and the project's own docs are candid about why:

SystemTaskPaper claimReproduced
mReFinEDMewsli‑9 disambiguation58.8% avg recall15.4% avg recall / 25.0% F1large gap
MVDZeShEL retrieval R@152.5%51.9%close match
BLINK7-dataset biencoder avg—≈82.7%on par
MHEL‑LLaMo (this work)4 historical benchmarksavg 0.608avg 0.59512/12 reproduced

The mReFinED gap is the most interesting finding, and it's diagnosed rather than hand-waved: mention detection stayed strong (62–91% gold recall) across all nine languages, but disambiguation collapsed specifically on non-Latin scripts — Arabic, Farsi, Japanese and Tamil all scored near 0% F1 while Latin-script languages (English, Spanish, German, Turkish) landed around 0.18–0.23. Some of the shortfall is a metric mismatch (end‑to‑end F1 vs. the paper's candidate Recall@1) and possibly a different checkpoint, but the script-dependent collapse looks like a genuine, reportable finding about how these multilingual encoders behave outside Latin script.

Data archaeology

The TR2016 dataset didn't want to be found

TR2016 (Tsai & Roth, NAACL 2016) has no HuggingFace mirror, no Kaggle copy, nothing conventional — and locating it turned into its own sub-project. The workspace holds 175+ root-level discovery scripts: scraping CogComp's live and Wayback‑archived publication pages, GitHub code search and grep.app sweeps for stray copies of the loader code, OpenAlex citation-graph lookups, and general web discovery sweeps. It eventually surfaced as xlwikifier-wikidata.zip (~268MB), hosted directly on CogComp's own server and linked from an internal resource page that standard search never reaches.

Getting it running wasn't the end of the trouble. A first pass reproduced only 2.8% macro recall against a published 28.4% — traced to 40–55% corrupted mention offsets in the source data (only ~48% of character spans actually matched their labeled text). Filtering those out lifted German alone from 1.54% to 8.42% recall, still well short of the paper's 28.2%, but it correctly separated "our pipeline is broken" from "the data we were handed is broken." A small self-healing deployment layer — an autonomous runner polling every five minutes plus a watchdog with restart limits — was built specifically because remote transfers to the GPU servers kept silently failing. The last recorded status (2026‑04‑20) found the eval code fully working on GPU (cuda available, ~14GB VRAM, 95–100% utilization) but the four language directories on the remote server still held only placeholder files — the real data hadn't actually landed yet, so TR2016 evaluation remains blocked on that last transfer step.

Why this matters more than it might look: the instinct to keep pushing on data provenance instead of accepting a bad number, and to separate a data-quality bug from a modeling bug, is exactly the kind of rigor a supervisor would want to see documented — even though the headline result (TR2016 recall) is still unfinished.

Deliverable

The "Final Entity Linking" package

Separate from the working repo is a compact, purpose-built submission folder — Final Entity Linking/ — assembled specifically to hand to a supervisor. It bundles a compiled thesis PDF (50+ pages), the XGBoost router model, cleaned result tables, five documentation guides (setup, usage, reproduction, datasets, structure), seven figures (architecture diagrams, per-benchmark F1 bar charts, the TR2016 offset-fix curve, MVD recall curves), and reading paths pitched at different time budgets — a 5‑minute skim, a 30‑minute review, or a full 2–3 hour reproduction walkthrough.

What stands out is that this package is more conservative than some of the working-repo status docs, not less: it explicitly flags a "1.0 accuracy" XGBoost result on a Mewsli‑9 subset as based on only 3 test records and calls it non-representative, rather than letting a flattering-looking number stand unqualified. That's a deliberate editorial choice for the audience it's built for.

More detail

Other facts worth knowing about

Prompt engineering ablation

A simple instruction prompt hit 65% success; adding context → 78%; adding a candidates list → 88%; adding chain-of-thought → 91% (but 2× slower); few-shot examples → 92% (higher token cost). The candidates-list prompt (V3) shipped as default, with chain-of-thought (V4) left as an optional mode.

Per-dataset model routing

Different LLMs per dataset/language: Mistral‑Small‑24B‑Instruct for HIPE‑DE (30 candidates, threshold 21.4), Llama‑Poro‑2‑8B‑Instruct for NewsEye‑FI, gemma‑3‑27b‑it for NewsEye‑SV (threshold 25), with smaller fallbacks (Ministral‑8B, gemma‑3‑12b‑it) for low-resource settings.

mReFinED compatibility fixes

Six concrete patches were needed to run a 2021-era model on a modern stack: CUDA 13→12.8 downgrade, tokenizer.encode_plus() → tokenizer() for transformers v5+, PEM dict/list format handling, forced single-GPU placement, absolute dataset paths, unbuffered logging. Full 9-language Mewsli-9 run: ~24 minutes wall-clock at 85–95% GPU utilization.

TR2016 offset fix, precisely

Fuzzy re-matching mention offsets within a ±5-character window took usable German mentions from ~30 to ~168 out of 2,000, lifting recall 1.54%→8.42% (5.5×) — still short of the paper's 28.2%, but it isolated a real data bug rather than a modeling one.

One loose end: the two server setup docs disagree with each other on the exact CUDA/PyTorch pin (11.8/2.6.0 in one guide vs. 12.4–12.8/2.8.0 in another) — worth reconciling before quoting a single version number externally.

Where things stand

Net assessment

Three things are genuinely finished and solid: the base MHEL‑LLaMo pipeline reproduction (12/12 within tolerance), the XGBoost confidence router (a real, measured +12pt improvement over a sensible baseline), and the AJMC‑EN error analysis. Two things are real but incomplete: the RAG/ensemble extension (built, staged, results not yet confirmed) and TR2016 (root cause diagnosed, infrastructure built, data transfer still the last blocker). One thing is a cautionary finding rather than a win: mReFinED badly underperforming its paper on non-Latin scripts — worth reporting as-is rather than something to fix away.

The volume of near-duplicate status reports, four separate vendored copies of the mReFinED baseline, and dozens of one-off scraping scripts are the visible cost of that rigor — this was not a smooth run, and the repo shows the scaffolding it took to get real numbers out of uncooperative infrastructure and undiscoverable data. The Final Entity Linking folder is the cleaned-up answer to that mess, and it's the one worth handing to a supervisor as-is.
